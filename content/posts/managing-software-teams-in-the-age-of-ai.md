How does managing a software team change when AI changes the way the work gets done? That question has been occupying my thoughts, and those of many other software engineering managers lately. Welcome to the first post on this blog, where I want to start exploring what that shift means for how we lead our teams.

Agile reshaped how we planned, communicated, and delivered. I believe AI may be the most significant shift in engineering management since teams began adopting the principles of the Agile Manifesto. Not because the two changes are identical, but because both invite us to rethink how we organize work. AI is again reshaping how work gets done and what it means for a team to perform well.

## Why This Moment Feels Different

The distinction I see is not simply faster work, but a change in who or what does parts of it. Past tooling shifts associated with the Agile revolution, such as version control workflows, CI/CD, and cloud computing, helped teams work faster. AI is taking on parts of writing code, tests, documentation, and even design options, producing drafts in minutes. But generating those drafts is not the same as deciding that they are ready to ship.

On my own teams, the bottleneck is shifting from writing code to preparing and reviewing work. We are planning sprints and then blowing through the planned work, while our regular cadence of backlog refinement meetings no longer prepares enough work to keep up. AI is reducing the constraint of how much code a developer can produce for a solution, and pull request reviews are becoming a bottleneck. Faster code generation has not removed the need to manage the flow of work; it has changed where that flow slows down.

## Norms Worth Questioning

Many of our management habits were built for a world where producing code was the slow, expensive part. If that constraint is loosening, we should ask whether the habits built around it still hold. A few I am rethinking:

- **Estimation and planning:** If effort for a task can swing from days to hours, what are story points actually telling us?
- **Measuring productivity:** What do output metrics like story points and the number of PRs tell us when AI can generate more code? How do we distinguish greater output from delivering the right thing, maintaining quality, and reducing delays?
- **Code review:** When more code is produced faster, review becomes the bottleneck. How should we change what we review and who reviews it?
- **Team structure and roles:** What does a healthy mix of junior and senior engineers look like, and how do people learn when AI handles the easy tasks?
- **Hiring and skills:** Which skills matter more now: judgment, system design, problem framing, communication, or is it just better prompt engineering?

## What Becomes More Important

If producing code gets cheaper, the scarce things move elsewhere: deciding what to build, validating that it is correct, keeping quality and security high, and maintaining shared understanding across the team.

Some of this is evolving quickly. The importance of security is rapidly becoming apparent as bad actors burn tokens to exploit software to their benefit. It could be argued that some of this is a result of the AI-generated software that our developers are rushing to approve PRs for. As things move so quickly, I have seen disjointed solutions emerge when the intended behavior and generated code were not fully understood. Code can be syntactically correct and still implement the wrong thing. The management challenge is to make understanding and validation part of the workflow, even when code generation accelerates.

## The Manager's Role

Managers cannot afford to wait for the answers to arrive. Our job is to set the conditions where the team can experiment safely, share what works, and be honest about what does not. That means asking better questions, being open about our own uncertainty, and revisiting old rules on purpose rather than by default.

This can be difficult to do on many fronts. First, as a manager you may need to reconsider long-held philosophies and once-proven approaches for new thinking. Anything that now causes friction in this world where AI is producing most of our code needs to be evaluated.

Questions I have asked include:

- How important is clean code when AI is producing most of it?
- Do sprints and related Agile ceremonies still provide value?
- How important is it for developers to know how to write software without AI?

I am not arguing that quality, maintainability, or shared understanding matter less. I am questioning whether the practices we use to achieve them still fit the way work is changing.

Once you can seriously evaluate these questions, the challenges continue in trying to convince your team and senior leadership that exploring alternative ways of working have merit.

## Where I Am Headed

I do not have all the answers, and I suspect no one does yet. In this blog I plan to explore what AI means for how software gets developed. I will share what I learn as I question these practices, and I want to hear what other managers are discovering along the way.

What management norms are you questioning on your team? What prompted you to question them, and what have you tried so far? I would love to hear your thoughts.
